    Scholar Taichiming Calls for United Nations Recognition of the Mong as an Indigenous People

    MongArchive Newsroom
    By MongArchive.org July 18, 2026
    Community News Recognition Request Editorial Review Historical Background

    Scroll

    Editorial Note MongArchive.org is an independent, unofficial platform for Mong research and archival preservation. This article reports on Scholar Taichiming’s public call for recognition. It is not an official United Nations statement and does not claim that the United Nations has formally recognized the Mong as an Indigenous people. The article will be updated if official United Nations records, transcripts, reports, recommendations, resolutions, or other verified documents become available.

    Scholar Taichiming recently appeared in a public video showing a setting connected to the United Nations and called for the Mong people to be recognized as an Indigenous people.

    His message focused on identity, history, culture, and the right of the Mong people to use their own name. He urged the international community to listen to Mong voices and examine the historical records shared by Mong scholars and community advocates.

    MongArchive.org has not yet confirmed the exact United Nations meeting, body, session, date, or official record connected to the appearance.

    The public video was shared by Mong Heritage on Facebook: View the Mong Heritage Facebook reel.

    Calling for International Recognition

    In his public message, Scholar Taichiming called for recognition of the Mong as an Indigenous people with their own name, history, identity, and cultural heritage.

    His request was not simply about changing a label. It was about protecting the Mong identity for future generations.

    For many years, Mong communities have faced confusion over names used by governments, scholars, missionaries, and outside institutions. Scholar Taichiming argued that the Mong people should have the right to identify themselves by their own traditional name.

    A Request for Recognition

    Scholar Taichiming’s public message should be understood as a request for recognition. It does not, by itself, mean that the United Nations has issued an official decision recognizing the Mong people.

    Official recognition would need to appear clearly in a United Nations report, recommendation, resolution, record, or other formal document.

    That written record is important because it could be studied, cited, shared, and preserved. It could also help researchers, educators, community leaders, and future generations explain who the Mong people are in an international setting.

    At this stage, it is accurate to say that Scholar Taichiming requested recognition. It is not yet accurate to say that the United Nations has formally recognized the Mong unless an official UN document clearly confirms that decision.

    Protecting the Mong Name

    A major part of this effort is the protection of the name Mong.

    Names carry history. They connect people to their parents, grandparents, ancestors, homeland, language, and memory.

    When a people lose control of their name, they may also lose control of how others write about their past. Their identity may be placed under a larger government label or explained through terms they did not choose for themselves.

    Scholar Taichiming’s work calls attention to the importance of self-identification. His message is that the Mong people should be allowed to present their own history and identity in their own voice.

    The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples addresses the rights of Indigenous peoples to maintain their identity, culture, language, traditions, institutions, and historical heritage. It also speaks against forced assimilation and the destruction of Indigenous culture.

    Historical Background of the Mong People

    The Mong people have deep historical roots in China and maintain a distinct language, identity, culture, and oral tradition. Over many generations, Mong communities also migrated into Vietnam, Laos, Thailand, Myanmar, and other parts of the world.

    Some Mong scholars and community historians connect the earliest Mong ancestors to ancient northern and central China, including areas near the Yellow River Basin and present day Shandong. These claims are important to Mong historical research, but the exact location and date of the earliest Mong origins remain subjects of continuing study and debate.

    MongArchive.org supports careful research into these questions through primary records, archaeology, linguistics, oral histories, migration traditions, and other forms of evidence. Claims about specific ancient populations or regions should be presented with clear sources and identified as scholarly interpretations when direct proof is not yet available.

    Years of Research and Advocacy

    Scholar Taichiming has spent years researching Mong history and sharing his findings with the public. He is the author of Mong China History and Heritage Preservation, a book focused on Mong history and cultural preservation.

    He has also served as president and executive director of Mong Heritage, an organization involved in Mong historical and cultural advocacy.

    His work has encouraged many Mong people to ask deeper questions:

    • Who are we?
    • What name did our ancestors use?
    • How has our history been recorded?
    • Who has the right to define our identity?
    • How can we protect our language, history, and culture for future generations?

    These questions are not only about the past. They also affect the future.

    Why Written Recognition Matters

    A public appeal connected to a United Nations setting can bring attention to an issue, but written documentation gives the issue a lasting record.

    For the Mong community, the next major goal would be to have the request and supporting evidence appear in official United Nations materials. This could include meeting records, reports, recommendations, submitted statements, or other formal documents.

    Written recognition could help open new opportunities for:

    • Historical and cultural research
    • Language preservation
    • International education
    • Museum and archive partnerships
    • Academic discussion
    • Community development
    • Protection of traditional identity
    • Cooperation with other Indigenous peoples

    It could also encourage major newspapers, universities, libraries, historians, and international organizations to examine Mong history more closely.

    A Historic Beginning, Not the Final Step

    Scholar Taichiming’s public appeal for United Nations recognition is an important moment, but the work is not finished.

    The public appeal has brought the Mong recognition request before a wider international audience. The next steps will require careful research, historical documentation, community support, legal understanding, and continued communication with relevant United Nations bodies.

    The most accurate way to describe the event is:

    Scholar Taichiming publicly called for United Nations recognition of the Mong as an Indigenous people.

    Clear wording protects the credibility of the recognition effort and the work of the scholars involved.

    Preserving Mong History for Future Generations

    Scholar Taichiming’s public appeal represents more than one person speaking for recognition.

    It represents generations of Mong people who preserved their identity through war, migration, political change, hardship, and displacement.

    It represents elders who carried oral history.

    It represents families who protected their language.

    It represents writers and researchers who searched through old records.

    It represents young Mong people who want to understand where they came from.

    Whether official recognition comes soon or takes many years, this public appeal has helped bring the Mong name into an international discussion.

    Scholar Taichiming has opened a door. The responsibility now belongs to the wider Mong community to continue the work with unity, strong evidence, careful writing, and respect for the truth.

    Looking Ahead

    MongArchive.org supports the preservation, study, and responsible publication of Mong history.

    We encourage scholars, elders, students, families, and community organizations to preserve documents, record oral histories, share sources, and support serious research.

    The goal is not only to gain recognition.

    The greater goal is to make sure the Mong people never lose their name, history, language, and identity.

    The journey is not over. Scholar Taichiming’s public appeal has brought the call for Mong recognition before a wider international audience. The next step is to preserve the record, verify the evidence, and continue the work.
